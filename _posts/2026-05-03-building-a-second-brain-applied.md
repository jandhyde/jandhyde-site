I read Building a Second Brain. Most of it didn't stick. What did is PARA, the framing of notes as packets sent to your future self, and one offhand line about how the research has to already be done. Below are the four times each one shaped a real decision.

PARA, briefly: Projects (things with outcomes and finish lines), Areas (ongoing responsibilities to maintain), Resources (topics of interest you might draw on later), Archives (everything no longer active). Forte's central claim is that the categories are exclusive in practice -- a thing you're working on is one of those four, not several -- and most knowledge systems get confused because they conflate them.

## PARA, applied to a life plan

January 2025 I was sketching a five-year personal plan and used PARA explicitly as the sorting structure. Projects were the things with outcomes -- a debt-payoff target, a WGU CS degree to finish, a weight target. Areas were the standing responsibilities -- Career Development, Financial Health, Knowledge Management. Resources were the bucket of topics I might draw on later but wasn't actively maintaining.

The clarifying part was the classification work itself. Forcing every item into one and only one bucket exposed where I'd been quietly treating ongoing maintenance like a project I could finish. You can't complete Financial Health. You can't complete Career Development. The only thing you can complete is a specific outcome inside an area -- which is what a project is for. The plan got noticeably saner once each ongoing thing was named as ongoing.

## PARA, applied as a diagnostic

Almost a year later I ran the same lens over the Notion workspace I'd been using for that plan and a lot more besides. The findings came directly from PARA's definitions:

- The Projects database had a Type property whose values included "Area" and "Resource." Projects, Areas, and Resources are different kinds of entities -- collapsing them into one database with a Type field was schema drift I hadn't noticed.
- The Areas/Resources database had a Done status. Areas don't complete by definition; the status existed anyway.
- "Move to..." pages and a "Blank Page - Random Stuff" page were sitting near the top of the workspace functioning as dumping grounds. In CODE terms (Capture, Organize, Distill, Express) that's a Capture-and-Organize failure -- inputs were getting captured but never directed to a home.

PARA gets pitched mostly as a structure for new systems. It's at least as useful as a diagnostic for ones you've already built -- "is this a Project, an Area, or a Resource, and is the schema treating it like one?" surfaces specific problems faster than any general "audit your tools" exercise would.

## Notes as packets sent forward

There's a marginal note in my Kindle copy that reads "Opportunities are notes to send my future self." I don't remember writing it but it's been the most active idea from the book in practice.

The same move shows up in three places I can name. A meeting recap I converted into a structured email for a colleague to send. A customer meeting transcript distilled into a Salesforce Seller Notes entry with the Current Process bulleted out, so the next person on the account didn't have to rebuild the picture. A mid-conversation prompt I asked for that packaged the entire state of a personal-finance categorization chat so I could continue it in a new thread without losing context.

All three are the same operation: take the live state of something messy, distill it, hand it forward to the version of you (or whoever inherits it) that has to act on it later. That's what a note is for. A note that doesn't survive transfer to a future actor is just a record.

## The research has to already be done

Forte makes a claim somewhere in the book that you can't start the research when the opportunity arrives -- the window between an opportunity showing up and needing to act on it is too short to begin reading. The inputs have to already be there.

I didn't credit the book at the time, but in retrospect it's exactly what shaped how I treated a GTM consulting offer in April 2026. I started building a reusable reference architecture, getting hands-on with Make and n8n, and treating the first engagement as R&D for a productized offering -- before signing anything. The bet was that the difference between "I'll start when the contract closes" and "I have most of what I need already" is the difference between a one-off engagement and the start of a practice.

Forte frames the principle as note-taking. It's broader than that. Whatever the opportunity is, the inputs have to be loaded before it shows up.

The framing of "building a second brain" is grandiose for what ends up being PARA plus a habit of capture. CODE is fine as a label but the work isn't carried by the acronym. The parts of the book on Express -- turning notes into output -- felt over-engineered for my purposes; the way I actually express ideas is by writing them somewhere visible, which doesn't need its own methodology. If I were recommending the book to someone, I'd tell them to read the PARA chapter and the Distill chapter and skim the rest.
