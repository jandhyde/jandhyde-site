There's a stretch I went through where the question "what else could I build with Claude" started showing up unprompted. I'd be in the middle of something else and notice I was already mentally queuing the next prompt. The output I was getting was real -- decks built faster, plans drafted on Saturday mornings that would have eaten a week, side projects suddenly tractable. It also felt like a treadmill.

The framing I had at the time was "am I addicted." That's the wrong frame. Heavy use of an effective tool and addiction look identical from the outside but are structurally different problems. The diagnostic isn't how much time you spend. It's what happens when you can't use it.

Two questions ended up being more useful than the addiction framing.

The first is whether you can defend the work cold. If a deck or a memo or a plan got built collaboratively with the model, fine -- the question is whether you could sit across a table from a sharp person and walk through every decision in it, line by line, with no preparation and no laptop. If yes, you used the model as a thinking partner and internalized the reasoning. If no, you outsourced the judgment and called it productivity.

The second is whether the dependency is on the tool or on the process. The tool can change, get worse, get expensive, or go away. The process -- thinking-through-things-with-something -- can survive any specific tool. But "thinking through things with the model in particular" can become a habit shape that's hard to perform without the exact same setup. That's the part to watch.

The other thing nobody tells you about being effective with these tools: the bottleneck shifts from execution to judgment about what's worth executing. You can now build almost anything in a fraction of the time. That doesn't mean everything should get built. The number of half-finished things sitting in my outputs folder went up sharply before I noticed this; the number of finished things didn't go up at the same rate. The constraint moved upstream and I hadn't.

The version of "what can I build with Claude" that's healthy is a curiosity question. The version that's an earworm is a dopamine question. The hard part is that they sound identical and only the second one shows up uninvited.

The move I settled on isn't using the tool less. It's deliberately working without it sometimes -- a meeting with no laptop, a memo drafted cold, a problem held in my head for a few hours before I ask anything. The point isn't austerity. It's verifying that the underlying judgment is still developing, not just being borrowed. Edges have to be yours.
