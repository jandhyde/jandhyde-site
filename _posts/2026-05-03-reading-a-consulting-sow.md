A working note on how I read a Statement of Work before signing it. Most of what's here came out of doing adversarial reviews of SOWs from both sides of the table. None of it is exotic. The point is just that the same handful of questions catch most of the problems, and people skip them because the document looks reasonable on first read.

**Calculate the effective hourly rate.** Take the fee, divide by the estimated hours in the SOW. That's the in-scope rate. Now find the out-of-scope rate, which is usually buried in a separate section. If the out-of-scope rate is lower than the in-scope rate, the consultant is mildly incentivized to push work out-of-scope, because every hour they move out is an hour they get paid for at the same or worse rate -- but it's an hour you have less leverage on, because you've already paid the in-scope fee. Normal practice is out-of-scope at a premium to in-scope, to disincentivize creep. When it's the other way around, you're going to get an out-of-scope bill, and you should be asking why before you sign.

**Count documents versus working systems.** Walk through the deliverables list and put each one in one of two buckets. A document is something that says what should happen. A working system is something that produces operational capability when the engagement ends. SOWs that are heavy on documents and light on working systems leave you with a stack of paper and the same workflow you had before. There's nothing wrong with paper deliverables -- sometimes paper is what you actually need -- but if you thought you were buying capability and you're buying paper, you should know that before signing rather than three months in.

**Notice when "acceptance" is tied to delivery, not usefulness.** Most SOWs include an acceptance clause that's effectively "the deliverable was handed over." That gives you the right to reject something for being late, but not for being superficial. If you care about substance -- and on knowledge-work deliverables you should -- bake substance into the acceptance criteria. Specify what topics the deliverable has to address, with enough specificity that "we hit every bullet but said nothing useful" is a rejectable outcome. This is unusual to ask for and people will push back. Ask anyway.

**Watch for the decision memo that's actually a rationalization memo.** When a deliverable is framed as "we'll evaluate options and recommend one," check whether the rest of the SOW assumes the decision has already been made. If the engagement structure, the timeline, or the procurement schedule presupposes Option A, then the "evaluation" is a rationalization for a pre-made choice. That can still be useful -- documenting why a decision was the right one is a real deliverable -- but call it what it is. Pay for what you're actually getting.

**Read it from multiple seats.** The fastest way to find problems in an SOW is to read it twice from different roles. The finance read asks "am I getting value for the money, and where's the perverse incentive?" The operator read asks "what does my team actually have at the end of this, and what's still broken?" The contracts read asks "what protects the other side more than it protects me?" These three reads find different problems, and any one of them done alone misses things. If you only have time for one, the operator read tends to be the most overlooked.

None of this requires a lawyer. It requires twenty minutes and the willingness to ask uncomfortable questions before signing instead of after.
