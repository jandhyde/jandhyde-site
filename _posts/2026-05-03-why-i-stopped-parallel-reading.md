title: "Why I stopped parallel-reading"
date: 2026-05-03
tags: [learning]
---

I used to keep three or four books going at once. The pitch I told myself was that I was breadth-reading -- letting different books cross-pollinate, picking up whichever felt right that day. The honest version was that I was avoiding the part of any one book where the new ideas stop and the slog begins.

What changed is I started Lean Analytics in the middle of doing work that the first two chapters were directly addressing. The book was clicking. And almost the moment that happened, I caught myself reaching for the parallel slot -- "I should pick up the next one alongside it." The justification was that they'd reinforce each other. The actual reason was that the book was about to require something of me, and a second book is a beautiful place to hide.

The argument against parallel reading is straightforward. Books that build a system spend two-thirds of their length building the conceptual scaffolding the last third depends on. Interrupting that with a different system fragments the scaffolding. You end up with a working memory of overlapping half-frameworks rather than one framework you can actually use. Surface coverage of three books beats deep understanding of one only if you're optimizing for being able to mention the books, which is a worse goal than it sounds like.

The argument for parallel reading is that interesting connections happen at the boundary between two systems. That's true, but only after you understand at least one of them well enough to know where its boundaries are. Two half-understood systems don't produce real connections. They produce vibes.

So now: one book at a time, finished, before the next one. The discipline is mostly about resisting the moment a book starts asking something of you. That moment looks like boredom. It is sometimes boredom. But more often it's the part where the frameworks have been laid out and the work is to start applying them, which is harder than reading.
