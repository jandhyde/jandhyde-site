## 1. Customer count is a vanity metric. Customer quality is the business.

Croll cites the B2B economics: top 20% of customers generate 150-300% of profits, bottom 10% can destroy 50-200%. Translation: cull, don't accumulate.

Where I've used it:

- Territory cleanup as an AE. Cut 5,100 assigned accounts to 3,800 (duplicates, defunct), then to 700 SAP-confirmed targets. My SDRs out-set every other team in the company.
- Conversion targeting model. Took the full renewing book through five filters -- eligibility, product fit, region, ICP score, focused goal. The cuts are the work.
- ICP weighting. Resisted equal-weight scoring across six categories. Industry and revenue are Tier 1 predictive; region is Tier 3. Treating them equally would have buried the real signal in noise.
- Account allocation across my team. Language match > geo > opportunity balance, in that priority order. Stops me from spreading attention evenly when even attention is the wrong move.

## 2. If a metric doesn't change a behavior, it's decoration.

Croll's question to ask of any metric: "What will I do differently based on this information?" If you can't answer, the metric is for show.

Where I've used it:

- SPIFF design. Caught myself building Q1 SPIFFs against assumed behavioral gaps before my team had even started the role. Restructured: SPIFF activity in one quarter that sets up the next quarter's pipeline. Same money, real behavior change.
- Conversion comp = signature, not implementation. My company has fixed implementation capacity per year. Tying comp to implementation completion would have paid the team on something they couldn't influence. I fought to define success at contract signature.
- NRR payout critique. Flagged a proposed comp structure where each percentage point of NRR triggered a $40-60K cliff. The marginal incentive curve was perverse. It wouldn't change anyone's behavior, it would just feel arbitrary.
- Pricing uplift pushback. When uplifts aren't tied to a customer behavior we want, they're noise to extract revenue. Sometimes that's fine. But call it what it is.

## 3. Acquisition-cost rules are amortization rules.

Croll's rule: don't spend more than 1/3 of a customer's lifetime value to acquire them. The math is the same as setting a price floor that recoups internal cost over the customer's expected horizon.

Where I've used it:

- Conversion price floor formula. For perpetual-to-subscription deals, I amortize internal conversion cost over a planning horizon, then add the customer's current maintenance with inflation. Different problem from CAC, identical structural logic. Larger accounts get smaller percentage uplifts automatically -- a feature, not a bug.
- Implementation cost evaluation. Same amortization logic helps decide whether a conversion deal is worth the professional services investment to land it.

## What I keep highlighting and keep failing at: focus

I marked three different lines about focus:

> "If you can limit what you're working on to just three big things, then everyone in the company knows what they're doing and why."

> "Virality requires focus."

> "Premature focus or optimization of things that don't really matter is a surefire way of killing your startup."

I have been doing the opposite for at least six months.

I built an internal invoice generator earlier this year that tracks team time against an SLA reference -- in-scope, gray area, out-of-scope. The reason I built it was scope creep. The thing I built does not solve scope creep. It makes it visible. There's a meaningful difference. Visibility is a precondition for saying no, but it isn't saying no. I have not actually started saying no.
